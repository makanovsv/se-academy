The Carestation 600 platform: models, assemblies and the gas path

Identify which Carestation 620, 650 or 650c and which options are in front of you, name its main assemblies and boards, and follow its gas from the inlets to the scavenging port, knowing which parts of the anes-l1 picture this machine changes.

Three badges, one machine

The Carestation 620, 650 and 650c share one gas path, one ventilator, one breathing system and one software family. The operator manual this course is built on is the User's Reference Manual for the A1 generation, software revision 01. The differences between the badges are mostly frame and features. The 620 is the basic trolley: one-pivot display arm, no central brake, no LED fresh gas readout above the flow knobs, fewer software options. The 650 is the full trolley with a two-pivot arm, central brake, drawers and the digital fresh gas display. The 650c is the same machine without a trolley, hung on a ceiling pendant or a wall rack, and it has no cylinder yokes and no auxiliary mains outlets. When a 650c is taken off its mount it no longer meets the stability requirements, so it is never moved as if it were a trolley.

Before you start, read the data plate on the left side. The serial number is coded: three letters for the product (SM6 for the 620, SM7 for the 650, SM8 for the 650c), two digits for the year, two for the week, a sequential number, a letter for the factory (W or M) and a letter for the generation (A for A1). That year-and-week code is not trivia: GE field safety notices for this family identify affected machines by exactly these fields. Then note the software revision from the start-up screen and the options: drive gas Air or O2, two or three fresh gases, second O2 inlet, cylinder yokes or DIN connections and how many, third cylinder, ACGO, auxiliary O2 flowmeter, Aux O2+Air, suction (vacuum or venturi), O2 cell, airway module type, mains outlets and isolation transformer. Several tests, alarms and spare parts depend on that list.

Your documentation set has three parts. The User's Reference Manual covers operation, checkout, alarms, user maintenance and specifications. The Technical Reference Manual is GE's service manual: calibrations, service mode, planned maintenance, repair and parts beyond the user list. A separate cleaning and sterilisation manual covers the breathing system. Match the revision of each to your machine's software. This course quotes only what the operator manual and public GE documents state; service values come from the Technical Reference Manual for your revision.

Gas supplies: what is behind the pressure numbers

Pipeline gases (O2, Air, N2O) enter through indexed fittings, each with a filter and a non-return valve. An optional fourth inlet takes a secondary low-pressure O2 supply, joined to the main O2 line through a shuttle valve. Cylinders, up to three, connect through pin-index yokes or DIN connectors; the primary regulator brings cylinder pressure down below about 345 kPa for pin-index and about 414 kPa for DIN, so that a full cylinder sits just below normal pipeline pressure and the pipeline is used first. A relief valve set at about 758 kPa protects the system downstream. The specified pipeline range is 280 to 600 kPa.

This machine has no mechanical pipeline gauges on the front. Pipeline pressures come from small sensor boards on each pipeline and cylinder pressures from transducers on each yoke, and both are shown on the screen. The supply alarm logic uses both signals: the O2, Air or N2O supply alarm appears when the pipeline is below 252 kPa and the cylinder is below 2633 kPa. A 'Cannot monitor … pipeline' message means the reading itself is invalid, a sensor or wiring question, not a gas question. So when a supply pressure looks wrong, first decide whether the gas is wrong or the measurement is wrong, with an external gauge.

The drive gas for the ventilator is either Air or O2, set at the factory and plumbed through a drive gas select point. If Air drives the ventilator, a second regulator reduces Air for the fresh gas control. Knowing the drive gas tells you which supply failure stops the ventilator and which only stops fresh gas.

Fresh gas: needle valves, Link-25 and the electronic layer

Fresh gas control is mechanical at its core. Each gas has a needle valve turned by its knob. O2 and N2O knobs are joined by a chain linkage, Link-25, that keeps the O2 concentration at the fresh gas outlet above roughly 25 percent in O2/N2O mixtures. N2O also passes a balance regulator whose control port is piloted by O2 pressure: as O2 pressure falls, N2O pressure falls with it, and N2O stops entirely in the range the specification gives as 3.5 to 27.6 kPa. Air is not affected by O2 pressure. Neither the chain nor the balance regulator knows what gas is actually in the O2 pipe; that is the job of the O2 monitor.

Around that mechanical core sits an electronic layer in the gas control module: a flow sensor for each gas, which gives the digital and on-screen flow readings, plus electrically operated selector valves for Air and N2O, an O2 secondary regulator, an O2 bypass valve and an O2 latching valve. The operator manual names these parts but does not describe their logic. It does state that N2O is shut off automatically when the Aux O2+Air switch is on, and that the 'Gas controls failure, O2 only' alarms come from a system communication failure (high priority) or a frame interface and power management board failure (medium). How the valves produce the O2-only state is per GE service documentation. A mechanical total flowmeter, 0 to 10 L/min, shows the summed flow independently of all electronics. If the screen and the total flowmeter disagree, the total flowmeter is your cross-check.

Two details surprise engineers coming from other machines. The O2 flow never goes fully to zero: with O2 displayed as zero, the manual allows some O2, between 25 and 125 mL/min, to keep flowing. And if mains and battery both fail during a case, O2 keeps flowing, the total flowmeter still shows it and the user can finish by hand. The flush valve sends a high O2 flow, specified at 25 to 75 L/min, into the fresh gas outlet, and a pressure switch tells the software whether the flush is pressed, which is how 'O2 flush stuck on?' is raised after 30 seconds.

Mixed gas goes to a two-position Selectatec manifold for Tec 6 Plus, Tec 7, Tec 820 or Tec 850 vaporisers, then to the ACGO switch if fitted. The ACGO sends fresh gas to a front outlet for an external manual circuit; it has its own pressure transducer and a mechanical overpressure valve (drawn as 110 cmH2O on the pneumatic diagram), and the specified ceiling at that outlet is 12.25 kPa (125 cmH2O) for flows of 25 to 75 L/min. In ACGO mode the Bag/Vent switch, APL valve, absorber and volume monitoring are out of the circuit.

The ventilator: a flow valve driving a bellows

This is the Datex-Ohmeda principle you may know from older GE machines, but check the details against this manual rather than memory. The vent engine sits in the middle pan under the work surface. Drive gas enters through an inlet valve and a filter, is reduced by a regulator to about 25 psi, and is metered by the inspiratory flow control valve, a servo valve capable of about 120 L/min. A drive gas flow sensor measures what it delivers. During inspiration the drive gas shuts the exhalation valve and forces the bellows downward inside its housing. During expiration a small controlled flow pressurises the exhalation diaphragm to give PEEP; the diagram shows about 2 cmH2O bias on the exhalation valve and a 3.5 cmH2O bias on the drive gas check valve.

A 200 mL reservoir and a bleed resistor keep the pressure on the exhalation valve steady, which is why you hear a slight hiss at high airway pressures: that is normal. A manifold pressure transducer watches the engine. A mechanical overpressure valve and a free breathing check valve protect the patient if the electronics fail. The operator manual draws the overpressure valve as 110 cmH2O on its pneumatic diagram and lists 115 plus or minus 10 cmH2O in the breathing system specifications, without saying which valve the second figure belongs to. The two do not conflict: 110 lies inside 105 to 125. For a service limit, use the Technical Reference Manual for your revision. Loss of manifold pressure control gives 'Vol vent only. No PEEP or PSV.': volume ventilation still works, pressure modes and PEEP do not.

Volume accuracy depends on the breathing system flow sensors, not on the valve alone. The ventilator measures delivered volume at the inspiratory and expiratory sensors and trims its output for fresh gas, small leaks and compression. When an alarm stops that trimming, the screen shows 'TV accuracy decreased. Adjust manually.' A flow sensor fault therefore looks like a ventilator fault, and lesson 4 shows how to separate them.

The compact breathing system and scavenging

The Compact Breathing System (CBS) hangs below and behind the work surface and drops down on a release button. The top part carries the bellows, its housing, rim and base manifold with the pressure relief (pop-off) valve that vents excess gas to scavenging at the end of expiration, the ventilator port, and the optional O2 cell. The bottom part carries the APL valve (about 0 to 70 cmH2O), the Bag/Vent switch, the inspiratory and expiratory check valves, the two flow sensors with their small flex boards, the patient port assembly, the bag hose port and the absorber canister on its lifter. A CO2 bypass lets the canister be removed during a case. Three optical sensors tell the electronics that the breathing system is latched, that the canister is in place and where the Bag/Vent switch is.

The flow sensors are variable-orifice sensors. Two tubes from each run to differential pressure transducers on the Anesthesia Control Board, and a third transducer measures airway pressure at the inspiratory sensor. Each sensor stores its calibration data in a small memory: when that cannot be read, the alarm asks you to replace the sensor. The manual specifies the whole breathing system leak as no more than 150 mL/min at 3 kPa, with or without the canister.

Scavenging is passive (with 10 cmH2O positive and 0.3 cmH2O negative relief) or one of several active AGSS versions with a 1.2 L reservoir, matched to low- or high-vacuum hospital systems. Active AGSS has a flow transducer, so 'Scavenging flow is too high/low' alarms exist. Sample gas from an airway module returns through the sample gas return port into the same scavenging path.

Electronics and integrated monitoring

Two computers run the machine. The Display Unit (CPU carrier board, 15-inch touchscreen, keypad, ComWheel, speaker, USB and Ethernet ports) runs a Windows CE 6.0 based application with its own storage. The Anesthesia Control Board (ACB) runs a real-time operating system, reads the breathing system transducers and drives the ventilator. Around them sit the power management board with the AC/DC supply and the sealed lead-acid backup battery (rated for about 90 minutes of full function), the frame interface board, the sensor interface board, pipeline sensor boards, an optional digital LED flow board, a transient suppression board at the mains inlet and the optional isolation transformer feeding the rear outlets.

Monitoring is partly built in and partly optional. The ventilator always measures pressure and volume. O2 comes from the optional galvanic cell or from an airway module (N-CAiO, E-sCAiO or E-sCAiOV) in the integrated slot; when both are present the module's value is displayed. The Data Source setting decides whether the screen shows ventilator or module values, but the ventilator keeps measuring and alarming either way.

What you will recognise from older Datex-Ohmeda machines such as the Aespire, Avance and Aisys: this manual names the same Selectatec manifold and Tec vaporiser family that the Aespire 7900 manual uses, a bellows driven by a flow servo valve, and CARESCAPE and N-CAiO airway modules with D-fend Pro water traps, which are GE's plug-in gas module family, not a Carestation-only part. GE does not publish that internal parts are shared, so the same principle does not mean the same part. Board names, connectors, software, calibrations and limits are this machine's own. Treat every older-machine habit as a hypothesis until this machine's documents confirm it.

field case

The machine is blamed, the cylinder is wrong

A workstation recorded in the field notes as an Aisys was reported as not working with a patient connected. Staff were convinced the machine had failed. The engineer on call could not get to the room and asked for a video call instead. On the screen it took only a minute to see the problem: the cylinder fitted to the machine for oxygen was a CO2 cylinder. The workstation had done exactly what it was built to do with the gas it was given. No self-test, no flow control and no ratio device on the machine could know that the gas in the oxygen path was not oxygen.

Supply alarms, Link-25 and the N2O cut-off all assume the O2 path carries O2. Only an O2 monitor or gas analyser checks gas identity. On a Carestation, as on any workstation, a functioning O2 measurement and a check of cylinder labels and connections are part of every checkout.

From service practice

A bag that filled itself overnight

On a GE Avance 5 the staff found the reservoir bag fully inflated in the morning on a machine that had been switched off overnight. Nothing else was reported. A colleague who knew the Avance pointed straight at the Bag/Vent switch block: a small rubber membrane that closes a port when the switch changes between manual and mechanical ventilation had cracked, and gas was creeping past it into the bag. The repair meant opening half of the block and fitting a new membrane. The same symptom appears in the Carestation operator manual: if the bellows fills in Bag or the bag fills in Vent, the manual names a leak through the Bag/Vent switch.

On Datex-Ohmeda designs the Bag/Vent switch is a valve, not just a sensor. Gas appearing in the side that should be closed points at its seals. Note it on a Carestation as a breathing system repair and run the full checkout afterwards.

On-site checklist

  • Data plate read: product code, year, week, site, generation
  • Software revision from the start-up screen recorded
  • Drive gas (Air or O2) and fresh gases (2 or 3) noted
  • Cylinders (yokes, DIN, third cylinder), second O2 inlet noted
  • ACGO, Aux O2, Aux O2+Air, suction type noted
  • O2 cell and airway module type noted; Data Source setting checked
  • AGSS type (passive or which active version) noted
  • Manual revisions (User's Reference, Technical Reference, cleaning) matched to the machine

Check yourself

1. A Carestation 650 has Air as drive gas. The Air pipeline is lost and no Air cylinder is fitted, while O2 is normal. What do you expect?

The drive gas decides which supply failure stops mechanical ventilation. With Air drive, Air loss gives 'Ventilator has no drive gas'.

2. The screen shows 'Cannot monitor O2 pipeline' while a wall gauge shows normal pressure and O2 flows normally. Where do you look first?

'Cannot monitor' means the reading is invalid. The gas is fine; the measurement chain is not.

3. With all flow knobs closed, the total flowmeter shows a small float lift on O2. What does the manual say about this?

The O2 flow does not go fully to zero on this machine, to reduce the risk of hypoxic mixtures at very low flows.

4. Which statement about the Carestation 650c is correct?

The 650c shares the gas path and ventilator but not the trolley features.

5. A colleague says the O2 cell is unnecessary because Link-25 guarantees at least 25% O2. Why is that wrong?

Mechanical proportioning cannot detect a wrong gas or a hypoxic mixture in the circuit; the manual itself says Link-25 does not replace an O2 monitor.
