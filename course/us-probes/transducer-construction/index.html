How a transducer is built: from the lens to the connector pins

Name every layer and component of an array probe from the lens to the connector, explain what each one does to the image, and predict which symptom a damaged layer or component will produce, so that every later test and repair decision rests on the construction.

Why construction comes first

In Level 1 you learned to treat the probe as the first and most fragile link of the chain and to separate a probe fault from a system fault. This course goes inside the probe. You will not build one, and in most cases you will not open one either, but every decision in the next seven lessons - what a tester map means, why a lens bubble matters, why a cheap reseal can ruin a probe, why a TEE probe is an electrical hazard after one bite - follows from how the probe is put together.

An array probe is a stack. From the patient side inwards there is the acoustic lens, one or more matching layers, the piezoelectric elements with their electrodes, the backing block behind them, the flexible circuit or wires that leave each element, the shielding, the housing that holds it all, the strain relief, a cable with one coaxial conductor per channel, and a connector with hundreds of pins. Some probes add electronics: switches in the connector, or a whole stage of beamforming in the handle. Each of these parts has a job, and each fails in its own recognisable way.

The active layer: elements, kerfs and electrodes

The heart of the probe is a thin plate of piezoelectric material, usually a lead zirconate titanate (PZT) ceramic or a ceramic-polymer composite, and in some premium probes a single-crystal material. A voltage across the plate makes it change thickness; a returning pressure wave makes it generate a small voltage. The plate thickness sets the resonant frequency: a thinner plate resonates at a higher frequency, which is why high-frequency linear probes have the thinnest and most fragile active layers.

To make an array, the plate is cut into narrow strips by a fine saw. Each strip is an element; the cuts between them are called kerfs and are filled with a soft, acoustically lossy material so that neighbouring elements do not ring together. A typical linear or convex array has from about a hundred to a few hundred elements; a phased array has fewer, narrower elements; a matrix array has thousands arranged in a grid. Each element has a signal electrode on one side and a common ground electrode on the other, usually on the patient side. The ground side is tied to the probe's internal shield.

Two things matter for service. First, the elements and the bonds between the layers are brittle. A drop does not need to crack the housing to crack the ceramic or break the thin electrode connection at the edge of an element. Second, every element is a small capacitor. That is how probe testers find a broken connection: an element whose conductor is cut no longer shows its normal capacitance at the connector.

Matching layers, lens and backing

Ceramic and tissue have very different acoustic impedances, so most of the energy would bounce back at the boundary if the ceramic touched the skin directly. One or more matching layers, each roughly a quarter of a wavelength thick and with an impedance between that of the ceramic and of tissue, let the sound pass. They are glued to the elements and to each other. When those bonds fail - from a drop, from heat, or from chemicals that have soaked in through the lens - a thin film of air or liquid forms between the layers. Air reflects almost everything, so a delaminated area becomes a weak or dead zone in the image, often irregular and often growing over months.

The acoustic lens is the patient-contact surface. It is usually a silicone rubber compound shaped as a cylinder to focus the beam in the elevation direction, across the thin dimension of the array. Its thickness and curvature set the elevation focus, and its material is chosen so that the speed of sound and the attenuation are known. This is why a lens is not a cosmetic cover. A replacement lens that is thicker, has a different curve or a different compound changes the focus and the sensitivity, even if the picture still 'looks fine' at first glance. The lens is also the first line of electrical insulation between the patient and the elements, and silicone is permeable to some chemicals, which is why disinfectant compatibility matters so much.

Behind the elements sits the backing block, a heavy, highly attenuating material that damps the ringing of each element after the transmit pulse and absorbs sound travelling backwards. Good damping gives short pulses and good axial resolution. Backing rarely fails on its own, but a crack through the stack after a drop often runs through it.

From the elements to the connector

Each element's signal electrode is connected, through a flexible printed circuit or fine wires soldered or bonded at the edge of the array, to its own micro-coaxial conductor in the cable. A general-imaging probe cable carries well over a hundred such conductors, each thinner than a hair, plus shields and a few control lines. All of them must bend thousands of times a day at the two strain reliefs, and that is where they break. A broken conductor gives exactly the same image symptom as a dead element: a narrow vertical gap at a fixed position. Only an electrical measurement, or flexing the cable while watching the image, tells the two apart.

The cable has an overall braided or foil shield, tied to the system's protective earth through the connector, and an outer jacket that is part of the insulation between the user, the patient and the conductors. A cut or crushed jacket is therefore both a noise problem and a safety problem.

The connector contains a printed circuit board with the pin field, a mechanical locking system and an identification circuit - a memory chip or coding resistors - that tells the system which probe is attached. Some probes also contain high-voltage switches or multiplexers in the connector or handle. These let a probe with more elements than the system has channels select which group of elements is active for each line. A fault in such a switch gives symptoms that look like a group of dead elements, or like a system channel fault, which is one more reason to test the probe on a second system before condemning a beamformer board.

Probes with electronics inside

Matrix probes and some advanced probes carry part of the beamformer in the handle: application-specific chips that combine the signals of small groups of elements before they enter the cable. That is the only way to serve thousands of elements through a cable of practical size. Such probes draw power, warm up in use, are sensitive to electrostatic discharge at the connector, and cannot be meaningfully tested element by element with a conventional probe tester. Mechanical volume probes carry a motor, a position encoder and sometimes a temperature sensor. TEE probes carry a temperature sensor in the tip and the mechanical steering in the handle. Each of these families gets its own lesson later.

For you, the important consequence is simple: the more electronics there are inside the probe, the less the field engineer can test and the less any repair outside the manufacturer or a specialised facility makes sense.

Reading a symptom against the construction

Once you can picture the stack, symptoms sort themselves. A narrow vertical gap at a fixed position is one element or one conductor. A group of adjacent gaps is a cracked section of the array, a broken flex circuit, a group of conductors at the strain relief, or a faulty switch. A broad weak area with a soft edge is delamination of matching layers or lens. Loss of sensitivity everywhere, with no gaps, is lens wear, a wrong replacement lens, ageing of the active layer or a system-side problem. Noise that changes with cable movement is a broken shield or conductor. 'Probe not recognised' is the connector, the identification circuit, the pins or the port.

The transmit pulses that reach the elements come from the system's high-voltage transmit supply; current pulser chips used in ultrasound are typically rated for pulses of up to around plus or minus one hundred volts. That is why liquid in a connector or a short in a cable can load the transmit supply enough to stop the whole system, and why a probe with a damaged connector must never be 'just tried' on a second, healthy system before it has been checked.

field case

Gaps that were not elements

A department reported three narrow dark lines close together on a convex probe. The previous engineer had read 'dead elements' and quoted a new probe. The next engineer repeated the air image and saw the same three gaps, then gently flexed the cable just behind the handle strain relief: the gaps flickered and, at one angle, disappeared. On another system the probe behaved the same way. The strain relief was stiff and slightly split. A probe tester at the repair facility showed the elements intact and three open conductors in the cable near the handle. The cable was replaced, the probe passed the facility's acoustic and electrical tests and returned to service at a fraction of the cost of a new probe.

A fixed vertical gap can be an element or a conductor. Flexing the cable while watching the image, and an electrical element test, separate them. That difference decides between a cable repair and a new probe.

From service practice

A convex probe that read as linear

A portable system sometimes failed to identify its probes and sometimes identified them wrongly: a convex probe appeared as a linear one and a linear as an endocavity probe, on both ports. The probes were original and worked correctly on another system. The engineer explained to the department that each probe carries identification data in its connector, which the system reads when the probe is connected. If the probe is fine elsewhere and several probes are misread on all ports, the read path in the system is the suspect. The system's connector board was found faulty and replaced, after which every probe was recognised correctly on every port.

Probe identification depends on both the probe's ID circuit and the system's read path; several good probes misread on every port point to the system, not the probes.

On-site checklist

  • Picture the stack before you judge a symptom: lens, matching layers, elements, backing, flex, cable, connector.
  • For every fixed gap, ask: element, conductor, switch or system channel?
  • Note whether the probe has electronics inside (switches, in-handle beamformer, motor, sensors) - it limits what you can test.
  • Treat a lens as an acoustic part with a defined focus, not a cover.
  • Never try a probe with a damaged connector on a second system before checking it.

Check yourself

1. A probe shows a broad weak area with soft edges that has grown over several months. No narrow gaps are visible. Which layer is the most likely cause?

A thin film of air between bonded layers reflects sound over an area: a broad, often growing weak zone. A single conductor gives a narrow fixed gap.

2. A cheap repair shop replaced a worn lens with a thicker one of a different compound. The image 'looks fine'. What should you expect?

Lens thickness, curvature and material set elevation focus and attenuation. A different lens changes performance even if the image looks normal at first glance.

3. How does a probe tester detect a broken conductor between the connector and an element?

Each element behaves as a small capacitor. A cut conductor disconnects it, so the capacitance seen at the pin drops.

4. Why can a probe with liquid in its connector stop an otherwise healthy system?

Transmit pulses up to about plus or minus 100 V reach the connector pins. A short there can overload the transmit supply and stop the system.

5. A matrix probe shows an image problem. Why is a conventional element-by-element probe tester of limited use on it?

In-handle electronics combine the signals of element groups before the cable, so the connector does not give direct access to each element.
